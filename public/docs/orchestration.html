System note LetsTalk / Elle · the interaction layer

The Layer Nobody Draws

Elle's orchestration: what decides when to talk, when to stop, and what to throw away. Roughly a fifth of the codebase, and none of it appears on the whiteboard diagram.

01 What orchestration is doing here

Draw a voice agent and you get three boxes: speech to text, a language model, text to speech. Ears, brain, mouth. Nothing in those three boxes decides when to speak.

That decision is a fourth thing, and it is not a box. It is a set of timers, gates and cancellation tokens sitting between the three, and if you do not design one you get one anyway, assembled one bug fix at a time. This document is what Elle's looks like after that process ran to completion.

Two files hold almost all of it. twilioBridge.ts owns the phone call: the socket, the audio clock, the barge-in decision, capacity, recording, and the session paired to a browser. deepgramCascade.ts owns the conversation: turn assembly, the tool loop, sentence-level speaking, and echo suppression. Between them, about 1,150 lines, against roughly 7,900 in the whole service.

02 One turn, end to end

The measured path from the caller falling silent to hearing the first word back. The endpointing wait is the largest single component and none of it is compute: it is the system deciding the caller is finished.

The order matters more than the totals. Speech recognition runs continuously, so transcription is not a step in this path at all; it has already happened by the time the caller stops. What remains is a timer, one model call, and one speech call.

Three of the optimisations in the diagram are worth naming, because each is a piece of orchestration rather than a faster vendor.

The reply is spoken sentence by sentence. The model streams, and a regular expression watches the stream for a completed sentence: at least fifteen characters ending in terminal punctuation followed by whitespace. The moment one exists it goes to speech synthesis while the rest of the reply is still being generated. Waiting for the whole reply would add most of a second before anything was audible.

The speech socket is warmed while the model thinks. Opening a websocket to the speech vendor costs real milliseconds, and those milliseconds are free if they overlap the model call. So the turn opens the socket for the language the caller is currently speaking before it has anything to say.

Utterances that arrive together become one turn. A caller who talks in bursts would otherwise produce a model call and a spoken answer per burst, and hear back-to-back replies to what was really one thought. Everything queued is merged into a single turn, logged when it happens.

Every row is a hand-tuned constant standing in for a judgment a person makes without thinking. That is the honest summary of what an interaction layer is when it is built out of orchestration rather than learned.

04 Barge-in, in three steps

When the caller talks over Elle and the gates above all pass, three things happen in order, and the order is the whole trick.

  1. Flush what is already gone. Audio has been handed to Twilio and is sitting in their buffer, ahead of the caller's ear. A clear frame drops it there, and the local queue is emptied. Without this she keeps talking for a second after being interrupted, which is the single most unnatural thing a voice agent does.

  2. Truncate the memory to what was actually heard. The model believes it said the whole reply. It said as much as was played. The bridge counts milliseconds actually sent and truncates the conversation history to match, so Elle's next turn follows from what the caller heard rather than from what she intended to say.

  3. Cancel the generation. The sequence number increments, which every in-flight stream is watching, so the model call, the speech synthesis, and the audio pacer all stop on their next check.

Step two is the one that is easy to skip and expensive to skip. Without it the transcript and the model's context drift apart from the conversation that actually took place, and the error compounds: every later turn reasons from a version of the call the caller never experienced.

05 The seam

All of the above describes the cascade, which is what answers the phone today. It is not welded in. The bridge talks to an interface, not to a vendor:

interface RealtimeModel {
  currentItemId: string | null;   // what is being spoken, for truncation
  appendAudio(base64ulaw)         // caller audio in
  sendText?(text)                 // typed on the paired browser, answered aloud
  cancelResponse()                // barge-in
  truncate(itemId, audioEndMs)    // memory follows what was heard
  sendFunctionResult(callId, out) // the tool loop
  greet?(text)                    // speak before the caller does
  close()
}

Three implementations sit behind it: the Deepgram cascade, OpenAI's realtime API, and Gemini Live on Vertex. The optional members are the interesting part of the design. A fused speech-to-speech model does its own turn-taking, so it implements cancelResponse and has nothing meaningful to do in truncate — there is no text history to trim, because the model's memory is the audio. The cascade is the opposite: its truncate is load-bearing and its "memory" is entirely text.

Which one runs is decided per call, read from the settings table rather than a cached value, so overlapping instances during a deploy cannot disagree. An override carries an expiry, so switching architectures for a demonstration cannot silently become permanent.

That seam is the point of the whole exercise. Everything in section 03 is compensation for a layer the architecture leaves out, and the version of this that is actually good will be a model that has it built in. When that swap happens it should be one line, not a rewrite.

07 The ceiling

Everything above is well-built and none of it fixes the shape of the pipeline. Three things are not reachable from here at any level of effort:

Prosody dies at the transcript. Speech to text hands over the words and drops the hesitation, the sarcasm, the rising panic. Elle answers the sentence. She has no access to the delivery, and she never will, because that information was discarded two layers upstream.

Overlap has no representation. The native unit of this system is a finished transcript. Two people speaking at once — the most ordinary thing in a real conversation — is architecturally undefined. She cannot murmur agreement while you are still talking, because the pipeline is strictly one turn at a time.

A timer cannot understand language. A person waits differently after "so anyway" than after "what do you think?". The endpointing timer hears the same silence in both.

Which is the argument for the seam in section 05, and for the fused models sitting behind it. Three layers buys a very good exchange. The layer that turns an exchange into a conversation is the one that has to be learned rather than tuned.
